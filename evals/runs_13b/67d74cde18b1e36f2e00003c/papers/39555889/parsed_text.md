# RankMHC: Learning to Rank Class-I Peptide-MHC Structural Models


## Introduction

Identifying which peptide antigens bind to class-I Major Histocompability Complexes (MHCs) and further elicit an immune response after being presented to T-cell receptors has been a longstanding problem in computational immunology.1,2 In recent years, great strides have been made in the task of peptide antigen identification, facilitated by the development of high-throughput mass-spectrometry experiments,3,4 which generate big data sets of amino acid sequences of MHC-bound peptides. These sequence-based data sets have in turn been used by Machine Learning (ML)-based tools, which are trained to predict pMHC binding.5,6 However, it is already known that there is an inherent structural dimension that plays a crucial role in the peptide-MHC (pMHC) interaction.7−9 Many experimental studies have confirmed structural effects and properties in the pMHC binding interface, such as important intermolecular bonds, or the solvent accessible surface, that are certain determinants of stronger MHC binding, better stability, or T-cell recognition.10−15 Protein structure databases such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB),16 or the IMGT-3D database17,18 are ever-increasing in size, with more and more experimentally determined structures being uploaded on a daily basis.19,20 This, along with recent successes in peptide-ligand docking tools21,22 and protein structural modeling methods such as Alphafold,23−26 have created an immediate need for developing successful and accurate pMHC structural modeling methodologies.

The field of pMHC structural modeling has started booming recently, following the increase in available pMHC structures in the PDB,16,19 with multiple pMHC modeling tools and methodologies appearing.27 Even though pMHC structural modeling approaches are quite varied in regards to the methodologies that are being employed, most tools fall under the paradigm of sampling and scoring.28 Specifically, given a pMHC as an input, many peptide binding poses are being generated as solutions in the output. The binding mode identification and ranking of different ligand poses that follows is a very well-studied problem29 and scoring functions are the most common way to rank an ensemble of ligand conformations.30 Typically, scoring functions are split in three categories: (a) physics-based scoring functions that involve force fields,31 solvation models,32 and quantum mechanics,33 (b) empirical scoring functions that use a linear combination of hydrogen bond, hydrophobicity, and potential steric clashes information, among others, to determine the energy of a protein–ligand conformation,34 and (c) knowledge-based scoring functions that employ statistical potentials.35 Most of the pMHC structural modeling tools in the literature employ such types of protein–ligand scoring functions. Specifically, Docktope,36 a web-based tool that uses a molecular docking/energy minimization approach to pMHC structural modeling, is using Autodock Vina,37 a molecular docking tool with an empirical scoring function, in order to dock, refine, and score pMHC models. Similarly, APE-Gen,38 as well as its successor APE-Gen2.0,39 which are two pMHC modeling tools that use a rapid pMHC structural modeling protocol, also employ empirical scoring functions such as Vina37 and Vinardo40 for refinement and scoring of the resulting pMHC models. PANDORA, a homology modeling-based pMHC modeling tool,41 employs the MODELER42 objective scoring function, molPDF, for ranking the different peptide loops that are refined by MODELER. Lastly, pMHC structural modeling efforts that are based on the Rosetta modeling suite43 have used different Rosetta-based scoring functions for evaluation, such as the Score12,44 talaris201445 and the newest ref2015 scoring function.46

During the past few years, a new category of ML-based protein–ligand scoring functions has emerged.47 Such ML-based scoring functions depend on large training data sets of protein–ligand structures and utilize nonlinearity, in regards to both activation functions and feature associations, to improve protein–ligand scoring and ranking. ML-based approaches have found much success in molecular docking,48−50 as well as in the related tasks of protein–ligand binding mode identification,51−53 protein–ligand binding affinity prediction54−56 and virtual screening applications.57−59 In regards to ML-based scoring functions for pMHC structural models, there has been a plethora of pMHC specific scoring functions designed to predict pMHC binding affinity,60,61 performing pMHC virtual screening,62 even for protein deimmunization.63 Focusing on the peptide binding mode identification task, GradDock64 was one of the first pMHC structural modeling tools that incorporated a pMHC-specific pose ranking module. The pose ranking function is based on a linear programming approach, where the weights are optimized on the following main condition: all the generated pMHC structural models must have a higher overall energy than the ground-truth native structure. However, GradDock performs only the aforementioned comparison of the native structure to the pMHC model, and does not perform pMHC model to pMHC model comparisons. Therefore, GradDock is not using relative pMHC structural model ranking information when optimizing the pose ranking function weights. Keller et al.65 have also created a pMHC-specific linear scoring function. They achieve this by optimizing Rosetta-derived energy terms on the Ligand Root Mean Squared Deviation (LRMSD) labels stemming from the distance between the peptide conformations from a pMHC structural model and the ground truth crystal structure. Their model of choice was a linearSVR, which exhibited better performance over other linear and nonlinear regression models that were tested.65 However, their resulting pMHC scoring function is only limited to the HLA-A*02:01 MHC allele, and only to nonamer peptides. The same linearSVR paradigm was also adopted by Gupta et al.,66 one key difference being that instead of using the LRMSD, the authors use the D-score to construct their labels, a metric based on the ψ and ϕ dihedral angles.67 While their model has been shown to generalize to non-A*02:01 alleles, it is still limited to nonamer peptides.

In this work, we present a new pMHC-specific binding mode identification predictor, RankMHC, trained to identify which pMHC binding pose from a pMHC conformational ensemble is the closest to the native structure. Inspired by the Learning-to-Rank (LTR) literature,68 we formulate the pMHC binding mode prediction identification task as a LTR problem. We demonstrate that, by using an LTR formulation, we obtain increased accuracy and better performance in regards to binding mode identification. Even though LTR has been previously employed in different structural protein–ligand related tasks such as virtual screening69,70 or prediction of allosteric sites,71 to our knowledge, this is the first work that applies LTR to pMHC structures specifically. RankMHC outperforms classical protein–ligand scoring functions, as well as pMHC-specific scoring functions, on different data set splits, on unseen MHC alleles, and can accurately rank peptides of different lengths. We further show that RankMHC exhibits good generalization capabilities, and can, under certain modeling assumptions, accurately rank pMHC structural models stemming from different pMHC structural modeling tools.


## Materials and Methods

Experimental pMHC structures were downloaded from the PDB.16 We follow the same process of pMHC structure collection as previously reported.39,41 Specifically, we removed from consideration any pMHC crystal structures that (i) resulted in parsing errors, (ii) exhibited missing residues on the peptide, and (iii) contained foreign molecules other than the bound peptide close to the MHC binding cleft. This filtering resulted in 566 pMHC structures that were used for structural modeling downstream.

For each pMHC structure we collected, we performed cross-docking for that particular pMHC pair using APE-Gen2.0.39 We followed the proposed modeling protocols when modeling pMHC structures with APE-Gen2.0. Namely, (a) we assumed the MHC to be rigid during the modeling process, as this previously resulted in better pMHC structures that were closer to the native structure,39 (b) we used Vinardo40 to locally optimize and score the peptide conformations, (c) we opted for the optional openMM72 energy minimization step, as minimizing peptide conformations has proved to result in more feasible and “protein-like” pMHC structures,39 and last, (d) we set the maximum number of pMHC conformations that APE-Gen2.0 would generate to be 100 (even though, in practice, some of the pMHC conformations are filtered out due to modeling inaccuracies such as fragmented peptides and peptides with severely misplaced anchors positions, among others). As a result of the pMHC structural modeling process, a pMHC structural database Q was created, comprising crystal structures, as well as their modeled counterparts. Specifically, for each pMHC native structure qi ∈ Q, we have a set of pMHC conformations of number Li – the subscript i in L referring to the fact that for each qi, we might a have a different number of pMHC conformations -, each having a measurable distance (measured in full-atom LRMSD) to the native structure.

RankMHC is an ML-based model, exhibiting an LTR-inspired architecture, and is trained to rank pMHC conformations by their closeness to a native structure. An illustration of RankMHC is presented in Figure 1. As input, RankMHC receives a ensemble of peptide 3D conformations that are bound to the MHC binding cleft. Through the featurization of the aforementioned conformations, and the ranking module that we have developed, RankMHC is able to provide a ranking of the conformational ensemble as an output. The conformation that has the best score and is ranked at the top is classified as the identified binding mode. In the following two sections, we will present an in-depth description of the two main components of RankMHC: the featurization module, and the ranking module.

RankMHC workflow. RankMHC receives as an input an ensemble of bound peptide conformations in the binding cleft, and provides a ranking in the output, with the top conformation being the most favorable one.

A necessary step in the RankMHC workflow is the featurization of our pMHC modeled structures after their generation, in order to provide a vectorized input to the ML architecture. We used the Rosetta modeling suite43 to extract energy-based terms for each pMHC model. Similar to previous works on pMHC-specific binding mode predictors65,66 and structure-based binding affinity predictors in the literature,61 we extracted per-amino acid energy terms for each amino acid in the peptide. These energy terms are the features to be given as an input to the ranking module of RankMHC. The energy function that was used in calculating and extracting the per-amino acid energy terms is the ref2015 scoring function, which is the default, and the latest scoring function in the Rosetta modeling suite.43 Additionally, inspired by GradDock,64 which extracts an expanded set of Rosetta-based energy terms, we also choose to extract an expanded set of Rosetta-based energy terms that are not used in the ref2015 scoring function by default. Finally, in addition to the Rosetta-derived features, we also used NACCESS 2.1.173 to extract the solvent accessible surface area (SASA), as well as the relative surface area (RSA) per residue. The default parameters of NACCESS, as well as a standard 1.4 Å radius probe for SASA/RSA calculation were used, following protocols as previously described in APE-Gen2.0 for the calculation of SASA and RSA.39 Taking the full set of features into account, but removing features that exhibit the same value for all conformations in the ensemble, we obtained a total of 776 features per structural model. The list of energy terms (either global or per residue features), with the accompanying description for each energy term, are shown in Table S1.

It is important to underline here that one of the goals of RankMHC was to expand ML-based binding mode prediction to arbitrary peptide lengths, as previous ML-based approaches – with the exception of GradDock64 – focused solely on nonamer peptides.65,66 As RankMHC uses an ML architecture74 which expects input features of a fixed length (see the Ranking Module section below for more details on the architecture of RankMHC), we needed a transformation that would convert peptides of different lengths to fixed-length vector inputs. Given the fact that nonamers are the most prevalent class of peptides that bind to class-I MHCs,75 we transformed each peptide to a nonamer.

To convert each peptide to a nonamer, we performed a custom average pooling process. An illustration of this process is found in Figure 2. The proposed pooling works as follows: first, we perform a structural alignment of the peptide in question with a canonical nonamer pMHC template deposited in the APE-Gen2.0 template database.39 The template selection follows protocols described in APE-Gen2.0; namely, we choose a nonamer template from the same MHC allele if possible, and as close to the peptide in question from a sequence identity perspective.39 Once the nonamer template has been selected and the structural alignment is performed, we match the residues of the peptide in question to the nonamer peptide distance-wise (see Figure 2). As mentioned above, RankMHC is based on LTR methodologies. Specifically, we use the LambdaMART algorithm78 as the backbone of RankMHC. LambdaMART combines RankNet/LambdaRank79 (see also Supplementary Text A for a detailed analysis on different LTR methodologies), with MART,80 a gradient boosting framework that uses regression trees and employs gradient descent during training. By default, we used a pairwise-based loss with no listwise scaling factor. Finally, we used the XGBoost framework and package74 for RankMHC, as it offers an efficient implementation of LambdaMART.

To train RankMHC, we used the above-mentioned pMHC database Q. We follow a nested Cross Validation (CV) approach for model evaluation and model selection. Specifically, for the model evaluation step, we partition the data set into train/test splits using a 6-fold CV. For the hyperparameter tuning/model selection step, we further partition the 6 training portions into train/validation splits in an internal 5-fold CV. The XGBoost hyperparameters that were chosen for optimization, as well as the hyperparameter values that were tested can be found in Table S2. Having selected the hyperparameters that lead to the best performance on the validation sets, the models were evaluated on the left-out test sets, leading to an unbiased evaluation.

In order to test the generalizability of RankMHC, we used different nested CV schemes, keeping the number of partitions and splits the same. The proposed schemes are as follows:Leave-K-PDBs-Out Cross Validation (LKPO-CV): The LKPO-CV split refers to random external/internal splits of the data set, making sure that no PDB code is found in both the training and test splits at the same time. This corresponds to the most realistic scenario of testing RankMHC to MHC alleles and peptide lengths that are previously seen in the training data set.Leave-K-Alleles-Out Cross Validation (LKAO-CV): Here, we perform the external and internal CV splits so that no MHC alleles from the test sets are found in the external/nested training sets. This corresponds to a scenario where RankMHC encounters a previously unseen MHC allele when trying to identify the correct peptide binding mode. This particular scenario is examined because previous studies have demonstrated loss of generalization for unseen MHC alleles.65Leave-One-Length-Out Cross Validation (LKPO-CV): Most previous ML-based approaches for pMHC binding mode identification focus specifically on nonamer peptides,65,66 as they are the most abundant in the pMHC repertoire,75 and the most abundant in regards to public data sets. Notably, RankMHC is designed for peptides of arbitrary length. Therefore, to test the generalization capabilities of RankMHC with respect to peptide lengths that have not been encountered during training, we perform the nested CV validation so that, for each split, the external test data sets contain one of the 6 available lengths (from 8 to 13 residues, which is actually the main motivation behind the proposed external 6-fold CV), while the external training splits contain all the rest. The same approach is also used for the internal 5-fold CV split.

To assess the effectiveness of RankMHC over different scoring functions that have been employed in pMHC binding mode identification, we consider several evaluation metrics, each one emphasizing different performance characteristics. Due to the combination of molecular docking and scoring with information retrieval methodologies in RankMHC, we opted to combine different evaluation metrics from the two fields. Below we describe these evaluation metrics in more detail, emphasizing on their different characteristics and goals.

Evaluating pMHC structural models using the LRMSD to the native pMHC structure is commonplace in the pMHC structural modeling literature.36,38,41 For this reason, we also adopt the LRMSD formulation, in order to evaluate how close the native crystal structure is to the pMHC conformation ranked first by each scoring function. Specifically, the LRMSD is defined as follows:N denotes the number of atoms in the peptide bound to the MHC cleft, and di is the 3D Euclidean distance between an atom from the peptide found in the pMHC structural model and the same exact atom found in the native structure. Here, we consider the full-atom LRMSD, including the atoms found in the side-chains of the peptide residues. To calculate the L-RMSD between the structural model and the native structure, we used ProFit.81

Most pMHC structural modeling tools emphasize the top scoring conformation as the representative and most accurate peptide binding mode. As such, we consider the LRMSD of the top-scoring conformation only (@1). Finally, as in our crystal structure data set we have many pMHC structures qi, the average LRMSD@1 of the whole data set is calculated, in order to assess the accuracy and efficacy of each scoring function.

The reciprocal rank denotes the inverse rank of the best pMHC model of the ensemble – best here denoting the model closest to the native structure qi – in the ranked pMHC conformation list. As an example, if the scoring function of choice places the actual best conformation as second best, then it is reciprocal rank would be 1/2. The best value for the reciprocal rank is 1 (where the best scored conformation is the actual best), with being the worst reciprocal rank value when the best conformation receives the worst score. The Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) is then calculated as a simple mean over the crystal structure data set Q:1

The Top-1 Precision (P@1) is an indicator function, returning 1 when the top ranked pMHC conformation is the actual best, and 0 otherwise. As such, the average top-1 precision is a number between 0 and 1 denoting the likelihood of the best pMHC conformation being identified by the scoring function of choice.2The factor is an indicator function that equals to 1 when the best pMHC model is ranked as a top-1 model.

The first three evaluation measures are based on binary relevance, meaning that they consider only the top ranked conformation to be relevant. However, for some cases, information about the correctness of the whole ranked list can also be useful.

The Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) measures the monotonicity of the relationship between two variables, defined as the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between ranks82:3where R(X), R(Y) are the ranks of variables X, Y, cov denotes the covariance between the ranks, and σ denotes the standard deviation of the ranks. Specific to ranking pMHC conformations, ρ can be employed to assess whether the scoring function is able to properly rank the whole ensemble of conformations.

As before, we are calculating the average ρ stemming from the different ρ’s we are obtaining for different qi ∈ Q. However, considering the average of correlations has proven to lead to underestimation.83 Therefore, we apply the following correction G(ρ) as found in Olkin and Pratt84:4where n equals to the sample size. Even though the transformation refers to Pearson correlation coefficients, treating ρ’s as Pearson correlation coefficients before transformations is considered robust.85 Finally, as the aforementioned transformation has a sample size requirement, we omit qi data points with Li ≤ 4.

The ρ metric assesses the correctness of the ranking, taking into account all the pMHC conformations of the ensemble. However, many applications, such as virtual screening, consider the top-k results of a ranked list of different targets.86 In such tasks, the weight falls mostly on the top-k ranking conformations. pMHC conformational scoring and ranking is no different, as the emphasis is mostly given on the top-scoring conformations for downstream tasks. The Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)87 is a metric that pays more attention to the ranking efficacy of higher ranked objects by introducing a logarithmic factor that reduces relevance according to an object’s rank:5where relj is the graded relevance of the object at position j. The Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) is simply a normalized version of DCG:6where the denominator is the ideal, maximum DCG that one can get by perfectly ranking a set of objects by their relevance.

Normally, relevance values in ranking tasks are integer values ranging from 1 to 5, with higher relevance being better.68 For the task of pMHC binding mode identification, we use the full-atom LRMSD values directly as relevance values when calculating NDCG. As LRMSD values closer to zero are better, we reverse the sign of LRMSDs to negative so that higher LRMSD is better. However, negatives relevance labels end in NDCG being unbounded. For this reason, we apply the following normalization as proposed in887This normalization of NDCG works with negative labels, and results in a bounded, 0 to 1 NDCG value, without sacrificing its statistical power. Additionally, as we have many qi ∈ Q pMHC instances, the average NDCG is considered in order to compare scoring functions.

## Featurization Module

A necessary step in the RankMHC workflow is the featurization of our pMHC modeled structures after their generation, in order to provide a vectorized input to the ML architecture. We used the Rosetta modeling suite43 to extract energy-based terms for each pMHC model. Similar to previous works on pMHC-specific binding mode predictors65,66 and structure-based binding affinity predictors in the literature,61 we extracted per-amino acid energy terms for each amino acid in the peptide. These energy terms are the features to be given as an input to the ranking module of RankMHC. The energy function that was used in calculating and extracting the per-amino acid energy terms is the ref2015 scoring function, which is the default, and the latest scoring function in the Rosetta modeling suite.43 Additionally, inspired by GradDock,64 which extracts an expanded set of Rosetta-based energy terms, we also choose to extract an expanded set of Rosetta-based energy terms that are not used in the ref2015 scoring function by default. Finally, in addition to the Rosetta-derived features, we also used NACCESS 2.1.173 to extract the solvent accessible surface area (SASA), as well as the relative surface area (RSA) per residue. The default parameters of NACCESS, as well as a standard 1.4 Å radius probe for SASA/RSA calculation were used, following protocols as previously described in APE-Gen2.0 for the calculation of SASA and RSA.39 Taking the full set of features into account, but removing features that exhibit the same value for all conformations in the ensemble, we obtained a total of 776 features per structural model. The list of energy terms (either global or per residue features), with the accompanying description for each energy term, are shown in Table S1.

It is important to underline here that one of the goals of RankMHC was to expand ML-based binding mode prediction to arbitrary peptide lengths, as previous ML-based approaches – with the exception of GradDock64 – focused solely on nonamer peptides.65,66 As RankMHC uses an ML architecture74 which expects input features of a fixed length (see the Ranking Module section below for more details on the architecture of RankMHC), we needed a transformation that would convert peptides of different lengths to fixed-length vector inputs. Given the fact that nonamers are the most prevalent class of peptides that bind to class-I MHCs,75 we transformed each peptide to a nonamer.

To convert each peptide to a nonamer, we performed a custom average pooling process. An illustration of this process is found in Figure 2. The proposed pooling works as follows: first, we perform a structural alignment of the peptide in question with a canonical nonamer pMHC template deposited in the APE-Gen2.0 template database.39 The template selection follows protocols described in APE-Gen2.0; namely, we choose a nonamer template from the same MHC allele if possible, and as close to the peptide in question from a sequence identity perspective.39 Once the nonamer template has been selected and the structural alignment is performed, we match the residues of the peptide in question to the nonamer peptide distance-wise (see Figure 2). Specifically, we view this process as an assignment problem. We are given a set of amino acids A from the peptide in question and a set of amino acids from the nonamer template T, along with a weight function C: A × T ⇒ R reflecting the Euclidean distance between two amino acid pairs. We are then searching for a bijection f: A ⇒ T such that the total cost function:is minimized. As additional constraints to the assignment problem, each amino acid a ∈ A is matched to exactly one amino acid f(a) ∈ T (in order to avoid multiple matches), and conversely, each amino acid t ∈ T is matched to at least one amino acid a ∈ A. The assignment problem can also be seen as a integer linear program. Some amino acids get grouped together in bins, corresponding to a specific residue from the nonamer template. ## Ranking Module

LambdaMART combines RankNet/LambdaRank79 (see also Supplementary Text A for a detailed analysis on different LTR methodologies), with MART,80 a gradient boosting framework that uses regression trees and employs gradient descent during training. By default, we used a pairwise-based loss with no listwise scaling factor. Finally, we used the XGBoost framework and package74 for RankMHC, as it offers an efficient implementation of LambdaMART.

To train RankMHC, we used the above-mentioned pMHC database Q. We follow a nested Cross Validation (CV) approach for model evaluation and model selection. Specifically, for the model evaluation step, we partition the data set into train/test splits using a 6-fold CV. For the hyperparameter tuning/model selection step, we further partition the 6 training portions into train/validation splits in an internal 5-fold CV. ## Evaluation Metrics

To assess the effectiveness of RankMHC over different scoring functions that have been employed in pMHC binding mode identification, we consider several evaluation metrics, each one emphasizing different performance characteristics. Due to the combination of molecular docking and scoring with information retrieval methodologies in RankMHC, we opted to combine different evaluation metrics from the two fields. Below we describe these evaluation metrics in more detail, emphasizing on their different characteristics and goals.

Evaluating pMHC structural models using the LRMSD to the native pMHC structure is commonplace in the pMHC structural modeling literature.36,38,41 For this reason, we also adopt the LRMSD formulation, in order to evaluate how close the native crystal structure is to the pMHC conformation ranked first by each scoring function. Specifically, the LRMSD is defined as follows:N denotes the number of atoms in the peptide bound to the MHC cleft, and di is the 3D Euclidean distance between an atom from the peptide found in the pMHC structural model and the same exact atom found in the native structure. Here, we consider the full-atom LRMSD, including the atoms found in the side-chains of the peptide residues. To calculate the L-RMSD between the structural model and the native structure, we used ProFit.81

Most pMHC structural modeling tools emphasize the top scoring conformation as the representative and most accurate peptide binding mode. As such, we consider the LRMSD of the top-scoring conformation only (@1). Finally, as in our crystal structure data set we have many pMHC structures qi, the average LRMSD@1 of the whole data set is calculated, in order to assess the accuracy and efficacy of each scoring function.

The reciprocal rank denotes the inverse rank of the best pMHC model of the ensemble – best here denoting the model closest to the native structure qi – in the ranked pMHC conformation list. As an example, if the scoring function of choice places the actual best conformation as second best, then it is reciprocal rank would be 1/2. The best value for the reciprocal rank is 1 (where the best scored conformation is the actual best), with being the worst reciprocal rank value when the best conformation receives the worst score. The Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) is then calculated as a simple mean over the crystal structure data set Q:1

The Top-1 Precision (P@1) is an indicator function, returning 1 when the top ranked pMHC conformation is the actual best, and 0 otherwise. As such, the average top-1 precision is a number between 0 and 1 denoting the likelihood of the best pMHC conformation being identified by the scoring function of choice.2The factor is an indicator function that equals to 1 when the best pMHC model is ranked as a top-1 model.

The first three evaluation measures are based on binary relevance, meaning that they consider only the top ranked conformation to be relevant. However, for some cases, information about the correctness of the whole ranked list can also be useful.

The Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) measures the monotonicity of the relationship between two variables, defined as the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between ranks82:3where R(X), R(Y) are the ranks of variables X, Y, cov denotes the covariance between the ranks, and σ denotes the standard deviation of the ranks. Specific to ranking pMHC conformations, ρ can be employed to assess whether the scoring function is able to properly rank the whole ensemble of conformations.

As before, we are calculating the average ρ stemming from the different ρ’s we are obtaining for different qi ∈ Q. However, considering the average of correlations has proven to lead to underestimation.83 Therefore, we apply the following correction G(ρ) as found in Olkin and Pratt84:4where n equals to the sample size. Even though the transformation refers to Pearson correlation coefficients, treating ρ’s as Pearson correlation coefficients before transformations is considered robust.85 Finally, as the aforementioned transformation has a sample size requirement, we omit qi data points with Li ≤ 4.

The ρ metric assesses the correctness of the ranking, taking into account all the pMHC conformations of the ensemble. However, many applications, such as virtual screening, consider the top-k results of a ranked list of different targets.86 In such tasks, the weight falls mostly on the top-k ranking conformations. pMHC conformational scoring and ranking is no different, as the emphasis is mostly given on the top-scoring conformations for downstream tasks. The Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)87 is a metric that pays more attention to the ranking efficacy of higher ranked objects by introducing a logarithmic factor that reduces relevance according to an object’s rank:5where relj is the graded relevance of the object at position j. The Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) is simply a normalized version of DCG:6where the denominator is the ideal, maximum DCG that one can get by perfectly ranking a set of objects by their relevance.

Normally, relevance values in ranking tasks are integer values ranging from 1 to 5, with higher relevance being better.68 For the task of pMHC binding mode identification, we use the full-atom LRMSD values directly as relevance values when calculating NDCG. As LRMSD values closer to zero are better, we reverse the sign of LRMSDs to negative so that higher LRMSD is better. However, negatives relevance labels end in NDCG being unbounded. Additionally, as we have many qi ∈ Q pMHC instances, the average NDCG is considered in order to compare scoring functions.

## Results and Discussion

As seen in Figure 1, RankMHC accepts as input an ensemble of pMHC conformations. The conformations are subsequently transformed into fixed-length feature vectors comprising global and per-residue energy terms. These features pass through the ranking module of RankMHC, which, based on the feature content, ranks the conformations in regards to LRMSD-based closeness to a native structure. To achieve this, RankMHC has been trained on a set of pMHC crystal structures and pMHC structural models created using APE-Gen2.0, a rapid pMHC structural modeling tool.39 Specifically, APE-Gen2.0 was used to construct a database of pMHC models, corresponding to different crystal structures (see Methods). The average LRMSD per-residue position for all the nonamer models generated by APE-Gen2.0 to their corresponding crystal structure can be seen in Figure 3. As expected, and also observed in other pMHC modeling tools and methodologies,65 the middle portion of the peptide is harder to model, while the anchor residues are easier, as there is a priori knowledge of their position in the B and F regions of the MHC protein.57 However, as also observed by the standard deviation lines (see Figure 3), there is sufficient variance in the LRMSD values corresponding to each position, in order to be able to distinguish between good and bad residue placements.

LRMSD distribution of nonamer pMHC models. Per-residue position LRMSD value distributions for all pMHC models generated by APE-Gen2.0.

We trained and evaluated the performance of RankMHC using the three proposed nested CV schemes (see Methods section). We compared the performance of RankMHC in these different nested CV schemes against different types of scoring functions that have been previously used for ranking ensembles of pMHC conformations: the ref2015 scoring function stems from the Rosetta modeling suite,43 and has been previously used in ranking Rosetta-based pMHC structural models;46 MODELER’s42 objective function, molPDF, is the scoring function used in PANDORA,41 a homology-based pMHC structural modeling tool; APE-Gen2.0 uses vina37 and vinardo40 to rank the generated ensemble of pMHC structural models.39 We also compared RankMHC against other ML-based pMHC ranking functions, namely, the one offered by GradDock,64 as well as the linearSVR ranking functions developed by Keller et al.65 and Gupta et al.66 For the latter ones, as the training data sets were substantially different, we opted in developing our own linearSVR, which was on the same data set and following the same protocols as RankMHC. Specifically, the 6-fold nested CV protocol was used to tune the regularization hyperparameter C, as described in Keller et al.65 We found a large agreement in the optimal values of C with the Keller et al. study.65 We also included in the comparison the structure-based pMHC binding affinity predictor 3pHLA,61 as well as the structure-based random forest model for pMHC virtual screening developed by Abella et al.62 This was done in order to assess how ML-based scoring functions, that have not been explicitly trained in the task of pMHC binding mode identification but in surrogate and correlated tasks of pMHC binding/pMHC affinity prediction, can perform in the task of pMHC binding mode identification. Moreover, for scoring functions that score the entirety of the pMHC complex and do not assess the peptide energy specifically, we subtract the energy assessment of the MHC alone from the score as previously described in the literature.61,89 Finally, in order to compare the different scoring functions in this benchmark, we used the evaluation metrics as presented in the Methods section above.

The results of the full comparison are displayed in Table 1. The first observation is that ML-based approaches, such as 3pHLA61 and the random forest by Abella et al.,62 which are not explicitly trained in the task of pMHC binding mode identification, but on a related task, are not effective in ranking pMHC models. This is evident by the very close to zero ρ. As far as classical, protein–ligand scoring functions are concerned, vina, used in APE-Gen2.0, is the most efficient in ranking pMHC structural models. Surprisingly, with the exception of molPDF, all general protein–ligand scoring functions surpass GradDock, which is a pMHC-specific ranking function. The LinearSVR scoring function outperforms general protein–ligand scoring functions on most metrics in the LKPO-CV setting, consistent with findings in previous studies.65,66 However, it is RankMHC which exhibits the top performance on LKPO-CV (Table 1). RankMHC is more accurate than both the LinearSVR model (p < 0.05 for P@1 using McNemar’s test, and p < 0.05 for the four remaining metrics using Wilcoxon’s paired signed rank test) and vina (p > 0.05 for P@1 using McNemar’s test, but p < 0.05 for the four remaining metrics using Wilcoxon’s paired signed rank test). When looking at LKAO-CV performance, the LinearSVR regressor loses a fair amount of generalization capability. This is also something that was observed previously when the LinearSVR was applied on alleles other than the HLA-A*02:01.65 RankMHC however retains the top performance in all evaluation metrics, highlighting the efficacy of the LTR training regime, and showcasing that LTR is a much more natural fit for ranking tasks (p < 0.05 for all metrics excluding ρ). RankMHC also exhibits competitite performance in the LOLO-CV setting, a result that demonstrates that RankMHC can be used for any peptide length.

Methods are categorized into three parts: pMHC-specific scoring functions that operate on auxiliary tasks that are correlated to the task of binding mode identification, general protein-ligand scoring functions that are not pMHC-specific, and pMHC-specific binding mode identification functions. The superscript * denotes statistical significance of RankMHC when compared to the second-best scoring method (assessed through McNemar’s test for P@1 and through Wilcoxon’s signed rank test for the other evaluation metrics).

It is worth noting that the results shown in Table 1 do not include redundant pMHC structures as these were filtered out during evaluation (see Supplementary Text B for more information on how redundant structures are removed). Results including redundant structures can be seen in Table S3. Even though P@1 and MRR are significantly reduced for all methods, ρ, NDCG and LMRSD@1 all remain similar to the nonredundant test set. We hypothesize that this happens because many of the methods identify a binding mode which is very close to the best one in regards to LRMSD, but not the actual best, due to the existence of redundancy. Most importantly, however, the relative performance of different methods remains largely the same, with RankMHC retaining the top or competitive performance throughout different metrics and nested CV schemes.

Given the success of RankMHC on the main comparison shown in Table 1, we sought to answer what are the determining features and design choices that contributed to such performance. To this end, we performed specific ablation studies that demonstrate the increases/decreases in the performance of RankMHC, given the existence/absence of specific features/design choices. Below, we explain briefly the different variations of the methods that we tested, but additional details and motivation for each variation can be found in the Supplementary Text B section.

To begin with, we wanted to test whether introducing/excluding redundancy in the training data set affects the performance of RankMHC. Removing redundant structures from the training data set reduces performance of RankMHC in all metrics (denoted as RankMHC – redundant structures in Figure 4A). The assumption here would be that overloading the training data set with data that exhibit the same pattern could lead to overfitting, hampering generalization performance and accuracy on the test set. However, RankMHC handles the redundant information in the training data set well, without losing in generalization performance.

Additionally, we wanted to test the usefulness of the average pooling approach that reduces peptides of different lengths to nonamer peptides. In particular, we examined whether feature padding with the neutral amino acid ‘X’ as previously proposed90 could result in better performance. When using padding instead of average pooling, the performance of RankMHC becomes slightly worse in three of the five employed metrics (see Figure 4A, model termed as RankMHC (padding)). The average pooling operation in this case results in a feature space of a smaller dimension, which does not compromise accuracy, while padding results in a larger feature space and a longer training process that does not necessarily lead to better performance downstream.

We also employed different feature sets to assess whether RankMHC benefits from the existence/absence of certain features. First, we created an instance of RankMHC where the features that are used are only the ones that stem from the ref2015 scoring function taken from the Rosetta modeling suite, excluding the additional features as seen in Table S1 (denoted as RankMHC – additional features in Figure 4A). The feature content without the additional features is similar to other work in the literature proposing pMHC-specific binding mode identification functions.65,66 The removal of additional features however resulted in subpar performance across all five metrics (see Figure 4A), demonstrating that the augmented set of Rosetta-derived features (see Table S1 and ref (64)) contributes to the good performance of RankMHC. Second, we experimented with a version of RankMHC that is not using any energy-based features from Rosetta (either the ref2015 features or the expanded feature set), but only uses peptide–peptide and pMHC interatomic distances (denoted as RankMHC – energy features in Figure 4A). Similar to the previous model, removal of energy-based features resulted in worse performance in four out of five metrics (see Figure 4A). This result demonstrates the usefulness of energy-derived feature sets compared to using the interatomic distances alone, even though the interatomic distances are used for computing the energy-derived features. Third, in addition to the per-peptide position feature set, we also included features from the MHC residues, specifically, the residues derived from the previously defined MHC pseudosequence5 (denoted as RankMHC + MHC features in Figure 4A). The MHC pseudosequence is commonly used in sequence-based ML models predicting pMHC binding, as using the MHC pseudosequence allows for generalization of such models to different MHC alleles.5,6 In our case, however, this resulted in worse performance in three out of five metrics, meaning that MHC residue features are not crucial to the performance of RankMHC. Finally, instead of per-peptide residue or per-MHC residue features, we attempted to introduce the pairwise features (denoted as RankMHC + pairwise features in Figure 4A) stemming directly from the pairwise interactions between the peptide residues and the MHC residues in the peptide-MHC binding cleft. The resulting feature set exhibits better performance than the baseline RankMHC in three out of five metrics. As such, if a user is interested in employing the top-k pMHC conformations from the RankMHC ranking, using the pairwise features might result in a more accurate top-k ranking, given the better NDCG and MRR scores. However, interestingly enough, pairwise features also exhibit the worst LRMSD@1 performance out of all the models that we created for the ablation study (see Figure 4A), making the model not particularly suitable if one is interested in the most accurate LRMSD. All of the aforementioned RankMHC instances are provided openly in the RankMHC github repository. Depending on the metric that the user is interested in, they can choose which model is the most appropriate for their downstream tasks.

We also experimented with altering the loss function of our XGBoost models. Specifically, we tested (a) a pointwise loss function that attempts to directly predict the LRMSD to the native structure, (b) the pairwise approach of RankMHC, and (c) a pairwise, but list-aware loss function, akin to LambdaRank.79 This way, we tried to cover all three facets of LTR (see Supplementary Text A for a detailed description for each of the three approaches). Results are shown in Table 2. Adopting a list-aware loss function does not result in better performance, with the pairwise approach exhibiting the best results overall. While initially this seems counterintuitive (as list-aware approaches use additional information from the whole ranked list), such results have been demonstrated before,91 hinting that the choice of the loss function in LTR depends on the downstream task, as well as the data set that is used. Moreover, what is also interesting is that the pointwise XGBoost is outperformed by classical scoring functions on the LKAO-CV and LOLO-CV, even by the LinearSVR, which is a linear-model based version of pointwise RankMHC. We hypothesize that this is due to the model overfitting to LRMSD values, instead of focusing on the ranking task. Pairwise and listwise approaches are able to partly circumvent this issue, by not emphasizing in accurately predicting LRMSD values, but the relative ranking of pMHC model pairs or lists. This is further evidence that the LTR approach as found in RankMHC is is more accurate and robust than previous pointwise approaches.65,66

Furthermore, we wanted to inspect specifically which peptide features used by RankMHC are the most important for the task of pMHC binding mode identification. We subsequently calculated the permutation feature importance for RankMHC. For each feature, we performed random shuffling of their instance-specific values, and reassessed the performance of the model on the test set. The random shuffling process was performed 50 times per feature, in order to reduce variance, and features that exhibited high collinearity (ρ between different features was used to assess collinearity) were removed from the analysis. We emphasized on two different aspects of the change in performance, namely, the change in the ability of the model to properly rank the whole ensemble of conformations (expressed in changes in ρ values, see Figure 4B), as well as the change in the ability of the model to identify the correct binding mode (expressed in changes in P@1 values, see Figure 4C). As a first observation, we see that in both Figure 4B,C, the per-position ref2015 score (denoted as total_score) proves to be a very important feature for many anchor and nonanchor peptide residues. This hints at the fact that the linear feature weighting as developed in ref2015 is already robust for the pMHC system, while also providing a good basis for further fine-tuning the per-position weights. This fact is also reinforced by Rosetta’s widespread use in the pMHC literature.15,45,61,65,66 Additionally, we inspected that, although there is overlap between Figure 4B,C in regards to which features are important, there are also distinct differences. Specifically, big differences in ρ seem to arise with permutations in features mostly related to anchor, or anchor-adjacent positions of the peptide, while P@1 performance reduction can also be caused by permuting features associated with the middle residues of the peptide, such as positions 6,7 and 4. From this, we hypothesize that different features of the peptide contribute to different aspects of the performance. We also observe that all the peptide positions contribute to the good performance of RankMHC, which is expected, as variance in the pMHC structural models generated by APE-Gen2.0 is present in all peptide residues (see Figure 3).

Finally, in addition to the global permutation feature importance as described above, we examined local, per-structure explanations, in order to assess the behavior of RankMHC in prioritizing certain peptide conformations over others. For this we used SHAP,92 focusing on particular examples where RankMHC correctly predicts the binding mode. One such example can be seen in Figure 4D. The crystal structure of the SARS-CoV-2 epitope (seen in green) exhibits a tryptophan in position 3 that is proximal to the α1 helix, and is substantially buried in the MHC pocket. We also visualize the top-1 and top-2 predictions by RankMHC. The top-1 prediction exhibits a lower full atom RMSD to the crystal structure (see Figure 4D), and it follows the native structure tryptophan placement, unlike the top-2 conformation which places tryptophan in a more exposed state, potentially relating to the elevation of the peptide backbone in the middle portion of the peptide. The SHAP decision plot can be seen in Figure 4E. We observe that crucial features that cause RankMHC to score the top-2 conformation lower than the top-1 conformation are the SASA/RSA values for the side chain/whole residue in position 3, the count of neighboring residues in position 3, as well as the ref2015 score for position 3. Specifically, focusing on the top-1 conformation, all the aforementioned position 3 features have a positive effect on the final score. However, for the top-2 conformation, position 3 features have a negative effect on the score relative to the top-1 score. This means that RankMHC does expect the tryptophan to be buried and in contact with the α1 helix, and penalizes the tryptophan placement if it is more exposed, demonstrating that there is a biological reasoning during RankMHC inference. It is notable that, for this example, both the ref2015 scoring function and RankMHC identify the correct binding mode, but this is not always the case. Figure S1A provides an example where RankMHC correctly identifies the binding mode, and ref2015 does not. Even though per-position ref2015 scores are important, and predictive of correct binding modes (see Figure 4B,C), the ref2015 scoring function as a whole provides an aggregate energy assessment for the whole complex, not weighting contributions of particular peptide positions/residues, which is a strength for RankMHC, as shown in Figure S1B. While RankMHC is not always correct at its top-1 assessments (see Table 1), the above-mentioned examples demonstrate some of the strengths of RankMHC as a pMHC-specific, per-position, pose scoring and ranking function.

All training and evaluation for RankMHC has been specifically on pMHC models generated by APE-Gen2.0. We, therefore, considered it important to test RankMHC with different pMHC structural modeling tools, and we opted for PANDORA, a homology modeling-based method.41 Specifically, we used the defaults parameters of PANDORA during modeling, with the maximum set of generated conformations to be 20 in total. We further excluded pMHC pairs that PANDORA could not model due to allele name mismatches. In total, 405 pMHC structures were modeled using PANDORA, generating 20 pMHC models for each pMHC structure. We subsequently used RankMHC to score and rank the 20 models for each pMHC structure. It is worth underlining that we did not retrain RankMHC with PANDORA-generated structures, but we used the RankMHC instance trained only on APE-Gen2.0 structures. This was done in order to assess the performance of RankMHC on models generated by a different pMHC structural modeling tool, with different modeling protocols. Additionally, as with the LKPO-CV experiment above, we made sure that the PDB codes in the training data were not present in the PANDORA test set, in order to avoid data leakage.

## RankMHC against Other Scoring Functions on pMHC Pose Ranking

As seen in Figure 1, RankMHC accepts as input an ensemble of pMHC conformations. The conformations are subsequently transformed into fixed-length feature vectors comprising global and per-residue energy terms. These features pass through the ranking module of RankMHC, which, based on the feature content, ranks the conformations in regards to LRMSD-based closeness to a native structure. To achieve this, RankMHC has been trained on a set of pMHC crystal structures and pMHC structural models created using APE-Gen2.0, a rapid pMHC structural modeling tool.39 Specifically, APE-Gen2.0 was used to construct a database of pMHC models, corresponding to different crystal structures (see Methods). The average LRMSD per-residue position for all the nonamer models generated by APE-Gen2.0 to their corresponding crystal structure can be seen in Figure 3. As expected, and also observed in other pMHC modeling tools and methodologies,65 the middle portion of the peptide is harder to model, while the anchor residues are easier, as there is a priori knowledge of their position in the B and F regions of the MHC protein.57 However, as also observed by the standard deviation lines (see Figure 3), there is sufficient variance in the LRMSD values corresponding to each position, in order to be able to distinguish between good and bad residue placements.

LRMSD distribution of nonamer pMHC models. Per-residue position LRMSD value distributions for all pMHC models generated by APE-Gen2.0.

We trained and evaluated the performance of RankMHC using the three proposed nested CV schemes (see Methods section). We compared the performance of RankMHC in these different nested CV schemes against different types of scoring functions that have been previously used for ranking ensembles of pMHC conformations: the ref2015 scoring function stems from the Rosetta modeling suite,43 and has been previously used in ranking Rosetta-based pMHC structural models;46 MODELER’s42 objective function, molPDF, is the scoring function used in PANDORA,41 a homology-based pMHC structural modeling tool; APE-Gen2.0 uses vina37 and vinardo40 to rank the generated ensemble of pMHC structural models.39 We also compared RankMHC against other ML-based pMHC ranking functions, namely, the one offered by GradDock,64 as well as the linearSVR ranking functions developed by Keller et al.65 and Gupta et al.66 For the latter ones, as the training data sets were substantially different, we opted in developing our own linearSVR, which was on the same data set and following the same protocols as RankMHC. Specifically, the 6-fold nested CV protocol was used to tune the regularization hyperparameter C, as described in Keller et al.65 We found a large agreement in the optimal values of C with the Keller et al. study.65 We also included in the comparison the structure-based pMHC binding affinity predictor 3pHLA,61 as well as the structure-based random forest model for pMHC virtual screening developed by Abella et al.62 This was done in order to assess how ML-based scoring functions, that have not been explicitly trained in the task of pMHC binding mode identification but in surrogate and correlated tasks of pMHC binding/pMHC affinity prediction, can perform in the task of pMHC binding mode identification. Moreover, for scoring functions that score the entirety of the pMHC complex and do not assess the peptide energy specifically, we subtract the energy assessment of the MHC alone from the score as previously described in the literature.61,89 Finally, in order to compare the different scoring functions in this benchmark, we used the evaluation metrics as presented in the Methods section above.

The results of the full comparison are displayed in Table 1. The first observation is that ML-based approaches, such as 3pHLA61 and the random forest by Abella et al.,62 which are not explicitly trained in the task of pMHC binding mode identification, but on a related task, are not effective in ranking pMHC models. This is evident by the very close to zero ρ. As far as classical, protein–ligand scoring functions are concerned, vina, used in APE-Gen2.0, is the most efficient in ranking pMHC structural models. Surprisingly, with the exception of molPDF, all general protein–ligand scoring functions surpass GradDock, which is a pMHC-specific ranking function. The LinearSVR scoring function outperforms general protein–ligand scoring functions on most metrics in the LKPO-CV setting, consistent with findings in previous studies.65,66 However, it is RankMHC which exhibits the top performance on LKPO-CV (Table 1). RankMHC is more accurate than both the LinearSVR model (p < 0.05 for P@1 using McNemar’s test, and p < 0.05 for the four remaining metrics using Wilcoxon’s paired signed rank test) and vina (p > 0.05 for P@1 using McNemar’s test, but p < 0.05 for the four remaining metrics using Wilcoxon’s paired signed rank test). When looking at LKAO-CV performance, the LinearSVR regressor loses a fair amount of generalization capability. This is also something that was observed previously when the LinearSVR was applied on alleles other than the HLA-A*02:01.65 RankMHC however retains the top performance in all evaluation metrics, highlighting the efficacy of the LTR training regime, and showcasing that LTR is a much more natural fit for ranking tasks (p < 0.05 for all metrics excluding ρ). RankMHC also exhibits competitite performance in the LOLO-CV setting, a result that demonstrates that RankMHC can be used for any peptide length.

## Ablation/Interpretability Studies

Given the success of RankMHC on the main comparison shown in Table 1, we sought to answer what are the determining features and design choices that contributed to such performance. To this end, we performed specific ablation studies that demonstrate the increases/decreases in the performance of RankMHC, given the existence/absence of specific features/design choices. Below, we explain briefly the different variations of the methods that we tested, but additional details and motivation for each variation can be found in the Supplementary Text B section.

To begin with, we wanted to test whether introducing/excluding redundancy in the training data set affects the performance of RankMHC. Removing redundant structures from the training data set reduces performance of RankMHC in all metrics (denoted as RankMHC – redundant structures in Figure 4A). The assumption here would be that overloading the training data set with data that exhibit the same pattern could lead to overfitting, hampering generalization performance and accuracy on the test set. However, RankMHC handles the redundant information in the training data set well, without losing in generalization performance.

Moreover, APE-Gen2.0 uses an energy minimization routine as a final relaxation step. In contrast, PANDORA does not include hydrogen atoms in the final models, and the resulting structures are not minimized. In order to confirm our hypothesis, we assessed the validity of pMHC models generated by both APE-Gen2.0 and PANDORA using MolProbity.93 PANDORA exhibits worse MolProbity scores when compared to APE-Gen2.0 (see Figure S2), possibly due to steric clashes, Ramachandran outliers or unfavorable side-chain rotamers.93 As RankMHC has been trained on pMHC models that have been relaxed through the APE-Gen2.0 energy minimization protocol, we assume that introducing a pMHC model with no hydrogen content and no relaxation will result in out-of-distribution data and lower performance.

## Conclusions

Identifying the binding mode of a ligand that is bound to a receptor is a very well-studied problem, and one of the main goals of molecular docking.29 The literature is rich with general protein–ligand scoring functions, which can be applied to many biological systems. Such scoring functions are based on well-founded theories and principles related to biophysics. However, those scoring functions do not inherently focus on characteristics that define a particular biological system, and as such, cannot utilize, without modifications, specific domain knowledge that is crucial in identifying a protein ligand binding mode or predict the binding affinity of a ligand to a protein. ML-based scoring functions excel on exactly these two things: (i) specificity to the desired domain of application (or, in other words, specificity to particular biological systems),56 and (ii) the ability to not be constrained to a classical scoring function’s predetermined functional form, to which many biological systems do not necessarily conform to.47 The last two points are especially important for protein–ligand interactions which are unconventional, or exhibit specific domain knowledge that can be utilized downstream, such as the pMHC system. Specifically, the anchor placement of the peptide residues in the MHC cleft is typically known, and the possible search space of peptide conformations is biased toward certain geometries. As such, there is a clear motivation on utilizing ML to advance pMHC binding mode identification.

This evidence is not new to the pMHC literature, with many approaches utilizing ML for better performance in the task of pMHC binding mode identification.64−66 However, such approaches mostly focus on direct, pointwise predictions of the distance of a pMHC model to a native structure. By being pointwise-driven, such approaches do not take into account information about the contrastive aspect of the problem, that is, that one pMHC model is closer to a native structure than in comparison with a different pMHC model. Based on methods that address such problems in the LTR literature, such as RankNet,94 LambdaRank,79 and LambdaMART,78 we created RankMHC, a new pMHC-specific binding mode identification predictor. RankMHC outperforms both classical peptide-ligand scoring functions, as well as other ML-based approaches in the literature (see Table 1). Results demonstrate that RankMHC can take advantage of both the domain specificity of the pMHC system, as well the pairwise, contrastive learning approach, to focus on the crux of the problem, which is the relative ranking of pMHC models. Using a pairwise learning approach, we focus on the relative differences between conformations, instead of absolute LRMSD values, circumventing this harder prediction problem altogether. Such an approach helps in improving performance and avoiding potential overfitting (see Table 2).

Another advantage of RankMHC over other approaches in the literature is that it is generalizable to multiple alleles and peptide lengths, contrary to other approaches that mostly focus on the HLA-A*02:01 allele and to nonamer peptides.65,66 In regards to peptide length, RankMHC achieves generalization by using a novel average pooling operation that converts any peptide to a nonamer with structural awareness (see Figure 2). As a result, RankMHC can adequately generalize to unseen peptide lengths (see Table 1 and the LOLO-CV results), and shows better performance than using simple padding (see Figure 4A). This demonstrates that RankMHC can be effective, using feature space that is smaller than a padded one, while being more efficient during training. We are curious to see whether such structure-aware transformations can be helpful in other systems that perform srtucture-based pMHC binding affinity prediction,61 or even help purely sequence-based methods that typically use padding90 or pruning of amino acids.5 We also plan to assess different ways of combining residues – average pooling being the simplest approach – and adopt more sophisticated pooling methodologies, such as attention-based pooling.95

However, it is important to acknowledge some limitations of RankMHC. In regards to RankMHC being used by modeling tools other than APE-Gen2.0, RankMHC achieves good results, only under certain conditions. In the case of PANDORA (see Figure 5), this involved a postprocessing step, comprising the addition of hydrogen atoms and the energy minimization of the whole pMHC complex. When RankMHC was used with the vanilla PANDORA-generated pMHC models, performance was subpar (see top half of Figure 5). We hypothesize that this is due to the different characteristics of PANDORA-generated pMHC models. Specifically, PANDORA-generated models exhibit higher MolProbity scores than APE-Gen2.0-generated models (see Figure S2), meaning that the PANDORA-generated models might contain steric clashes, Ramachandran outliers or unfavorable side-chain rotamers.93 This, in turn, can change the feature content of pMHC models so much that they become out-of-distribution data points for RankMHC. As future work, to avoid potential out-of-distribution issues, we plan to expand the training data set of RankMHC, using pMHC models generated by different pMHC structural modeling tools that employ different methodologies. As such methodologies search the peptide conformational space in different ways, the geometries across different pMHC tools may vary, and such variety in the training data can be beneficial for RankMHC performance. Furthermore, as seen in Figure 3, even though there is enough variability per residue position in the RankMHC training data set of APE-Gen2.0-generated structures, pMHC models are biased in regards to their geometries. For instance, no peptide that exhibits severely misplaced anchor positions, or is not placed inside the MHC cleft at all, is included in the structural database that was used to train RankMHC. Therefore, such a peptide geometry would also be an out-of-distribution data point for RankMHC, as RankMHC was not trained on such structures. However, such examples hardly ever occur in practice with regard to pMHC modeling, as most pMHC modeling tools are either template-based,39,41,66,96 or would include distance-based thresholds which filter out peptides that exhibit bad geometries.38,39

We would also like to acknowledge that RankMHC has been trained on a specific task, namely pMHC binding mode identification. It is not designed for auxiliary tasks such as pMHC binding affinity prediction or virtual screening applications. The seminal work by Ashtawy and Mahapatra expands more on this topic, showing through benchmarks that task-specific, but even more general scoring functions cannot generalize equally well to different tasks.97 In our experiments, we also observed similar behavior with 3pHLA61 and the random forest model by Abella et al.62 not being able to properly rank peptide conformations. However, the work by Ashtawy and Mahapatra also proposes as a solution a multitask traning approach, where there is one system that shares information for three different tasks – in the paper, the authors discuss molecular docking, virtual screening and binding mode identification – which are correlated with each another.97 This notion of multitask learning has been adopted by other ML-based scoring functions, such as GNINA, which is trained on both protein–ligand binding affinity labels, as well as labels related to the appropriate binding mode.50,98 Therefore, future work will also focus on combining knowledge from RankMHC and our previously developed structural pMHC tools for binding affinity prediction61 and virtual screening.62 We will consider building a pMHC multitask system that shares structural information between different tasks, in order to improve performance, as the tasks of pMHC binding affinity prediction, binding mode identification and virtual screening are, to some extent, correlated.
